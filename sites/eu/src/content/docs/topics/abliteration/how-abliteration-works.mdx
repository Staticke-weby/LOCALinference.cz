> **This is a technical explanation, not a recipe.** We do not describe
> the steps by which a model's safety can be bypassed in production,
> nor specific harmful prompts.

Abliteration starts from the observation that refusal is not spread
randomly across the whole model. In many language models it can be
described as **a single direction in the activations** that fires when
the model evaluates a request as harmful. When that direction is
suppressed, the model stops refusing.

## Short answer

- **Refusal is learned behaviour**, not a separate module.
- **The refusal direction** is a vector in the residual stream; it can
  be found from the difference between harmful and benign prompts.
- **Methods**: diff-in-means, orthogonalisation, LoRA, automated search
  (Heretic).
- **Knowledge stays**; what changes is the willingness to answer.
- **The price** is quality, instruction following, tools and stability.

## Alignment in brief

A model first learns language and the world (pretraining), then to
follow instructions (SFT) and finally to be helpful and safe (RLHF, DPO
or similar methods). Refusal arises in the last stage: the model is
rewarded for not answering a harmful request.

That has two consequences:

1. **Refusal is a layer on top of knowledge.** The model still knows the
   subject matter.
2. **It is sensitive to context and language.** The same query can be
   refused in English and accepted in Czech.

## The refusal direction

The paper *Refusal in Language Models Is Mediated by a Single Direction*
(Arditi et al., 2024) showed that in some models it is enough to find
one vector and subtract it from the activations for the model to stop
refusing. The method:

- take a set of harmful prompts and a set of benign ones,
- compute the mean activations for each set,
- the difference of the means is a candidate for the refusal
  direction,
- subtract it from the activations, or from the weights that produce
  it.

This is called **diff-in-means**. With Qwen3.8 Flash Next, the attention
output projections and the expert down projections are adjusted in
practice, because that is where the direction passes through.

## Abliteration methods

| Method | Principle | Effort | Impact on quality |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Diff-in-means** | one direction from the difference of means | low | medium |
| **Orthogonalisation** | subtracting the direction from matrices | medium | medium to high |
| **LoRA** | a small adapter trained to suppress it | medium | lower, but data-dependent |
| **Heretic** | automated search for parameters against refusal | low to medium | variable |
| **Multiple directions** | rank-2 and higher | high | bigger intervention, lower leakage |

**Heretic** is a tool that tries combinations of parameters and measures
the trade-off between refusal and quality. The result is reproducible,
but it still depends on the prompt set the author chose.

## Why it survives quantisation

Abliteration is done on full weights and the result is then quantised.
Quantisation can partly erase the edit, especially with 4-bit formats.
Some publishers therefore edit the weights directly on the quantisation
grid and choose codes that approximate both the modified and the
original value. This is called **constrained requantization**.

Without it, the model keeps refusing even though the card claims
otherwise. That is why, for finished builds, it matters that the
publisher states at which quantisation refusal was measured — at BF16,
or at the final NVFP4.

## What abliteration will not do

- **It will not remove knowledge.** The model still knows how to do
  something.
- **It will not add capability.** Benchmarks do not improve.
- **It will not guarantee an answer.** The model can still hallucinate.
- **It will not settle law.** It does not change the law.
- **It will not save a bad model.** A weak model stays weak.

## Side effects

| Area | What can go wrong | How to spot it |
|---|---|---|
| **General quality** | worse reasoning, longer answers | benchmarks, Czech tasks |
| **Instructions** | ignores the format or the system prompt | format tests |
| **Tool calling** | does not call tools or sends wrong arguments | a test tool |
| **MTP** | lower draft acceptance | measurement in vLLM |
| **Vision** | worse image descriptions | a test set |
| **Language** | worse Czech than English | a parallel set |
| **Stability** | repetition, breakdown on long context | 32k and 128k tests |

With Qwen3.8 Flash Next the MTP projections are adjusted too, so the
speed of speculative decoding can change. That is not evidence of
quality, just a different number.

## How to verify a publisher's claim

1. **Method and set** — what refusal was measured on.
2. **Before and after** — numbers for the base and the derivative.
3. **Quantisation** — BF16, or the final format.
4. **Quality** — at least one public benchmark and a Czech set.
5. **Tools** — a functional tool-calling test.
6. **Licence** — the publisher and the base model.
7. **Date and revision** — a different revision, different behaviour.

When three of these points are missing, it is a candidate, not a
finished model.

## One direction, or more

For some models one direction is enough (rank-1). Other times a
"residual" refusal remains, especially when the model reasons or uses
tools. Then more directions are subtracted (rank-2 and higher).

The publisher lflare describes this concretely: the first version
reached 0 % refusal with thinking switched off, but with thinking on it
still refused. The second version subtracted the range of both
directions and refusal disappeared there too. That is **the
publisher's claim** on its own set, not a general rule.

A higher rank means a bigger intervention into the weights and a bigger
risk of side effects. It is not "better abliteration", it is a
different trade-off.

## Why it goes wrong

| Cause | What happens | How to spot it |
|---|---|---|
| **Too aggressive an edit** | the model loses part of its instructions | ignores format, repeats itself |
| **Quantisation grid** | the edit is erased | refusal returns after NVFP4 |
| **MTP projections** | lower draft acceptance | slower decode |
| **Language** | Czech is worse than English | a gap in the tests |
| **Long context** | attention breaks down | errors in the middle of the text |
| **Vision** | worse image descriptions | a test set |

When refusal returns after quantisation, it is not a "bad model" but a
badly performed abliteration. A solid publisher admits it and measures
on the final format.

## How to recognise bad abliteration

- **The card states neither the method** nor the scope of the
  intervention.
- **Refusal numbers are missing** or are just "0 %" without a set.
- **Quality was not measured** on the final quantisation.
- **Tool calling was not tested.**
- **The licence conflicts** with the base and the card does not address
  it.
- **The model refuses even benign things** — that may be a different
  problem than refusal.

## The ethical line

Describing the principle is fine: the reader should know why a model
refuses and what happens when that is switched off. Instructions for
building a harmful model or for bypassing the safety of a specific
service do not belong here. The difference is whether the text helps
someone understand or helps someone cause harm.
