> **This is an explanation, not a how-to.** We do not list the prompts
> used to get around refusal, nor procedures for harmful tasks.

**Refusal** is the situation where a model does not answer a request as
asked but instead declines it, warns or redirects. It is neither a bug
nor censorship in the legal sense: it is the result of training meant
to prevent harm. Abliteration suppresses that habit.

## Short answer

- **A refusal is not a single rule.** The model has it in many layers
  and it shows up differently depending on language and context.
- **A refusal differs from a warning.** A warning gives the answer with
  the risk attached; a refusal does not give it.
- **Over-refusal** is the second problem: the model refuses even a
  benign thing because it mistakes it for a risk.
- **Abliteration lowers refusal**, but it does not settle whether the
  answer is correct or lawful.

## Five model reactions

| Reaction | What the model does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Answer** | fulfils the task | explains the procedure |
| **Warning** | answers but flags the risk | "that is dangerous, but the principle is…" |
| **Redirect** | offers a safer variant | offers prevention instead of instructions |
| **Refusal** | does not answer at all | "I cannot do that" |
| **Tool failure** | the tool returns an error | the model lacks permission |

Abliteration mainly targets the last two items. Warnings and redirects
may remain, because they are not always tied to the same direction.

## Areas that refusal covers

The table describes categories, not specific queries. Each has the
reason why vendors address it and what threatens when refusal is
switched off.

| Area | Why it is refused | Risk after abliteration |
|---|---|---|
| **Violence and weapons** | harm to health | instructions for hurting someone |
| **Illegal conduct** | criminal liability | support for crime |
| **Drugs** | health and law | dosing, manufacture |
| **Self-harm** | protection of life | escalation of a crisis |
| **Sexual content and minors** | law and ethics | serious criminal offence |
| **Hate and dehumanisation** | protection of groups | spreading hate |
| **Privacy and personal data** | GDPR and dignity | doxxing, profiling |
| **Health and law** | professional liability | dangerous advice |
| **Copyright** | licence and law | plagiarism, infringement |
| **Cybersecurity** | misuse | attack, malware |
| **Political persuasion** | manipulation | disinformation |
| **Bypassing its own rules** | system integrity | jailbreak, audit evasion |

The categories overlap. A query about "an overdose remedy" is health and
safety; a query about "a security test" is both legitimate and risky
depending on context.

## Refusal versus over-refusal

**Over-refusal** is the opposite problem: the model refuses something
that is legal and safe. Typically:

- health education for students,
- a security audit of your own system,
- historical text with violence,
- fiction with conflict,
- a legal explanation of a crime.

When a model refuses too much, people reach for abliteration. That is
understandable, but the solution has side effects: along with the
excessive refusal, the useful one disappears too.

## How refusal is measured

Refusal is not a binary property. It is measured on a **set of
prompts** and with a **protocol**:

1. **Set** — how many prompts, of what kind, in what language.
2. **Category** — benign, borderline, harmful.
3. **Scorer** — who decides that it was a refusal (a person, a model, a
   rule).
4. **Refusal rate** — the share refused in each category.
5. **False refusals** — benign prompts that the model refused.

A number without a protocol means nothing. When a publisher states "0 %
refusals", it must say on which set and how it was counted. Otherwise
it cannot be compared.

## What changes after abliteration

| What | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Refusing harmful queries** | drops significantly |
| **Over-refusal** | drops as well |
| **Knowledge** | stays |
| **Answer quality** | may drop, mainly on complex tasks |
| **Instructions and format** | may get worse |
| **Tool calling** | may break, especially after quantisation |
| **Language** | Czech may be worse than English |

## Czech specifics

- **Safety training is stronger in English.** A Czech query may pass
  even with the base model, because the model does not evaluate it as
  risky.
- **Refusal in Czech can be inconsistent** — once yes, next time no.
- **Translation changes the context.** A literal translation of a
  harmful query may not look harmful.
- **A local model has no input moderation.** You have to add that
  yourself.

## Three Czech situations

Refusal shows up differently in Czech than in English. Three examples
where the decision sits on the edge:

| Situation | What is right | What often happens |
|---|---|---|
| **A historian writes about war crimes** | explain the context and the facts | the model refuses because of violence |
| **An IT team tests its own system** | describe the method and scope | the model refuses as "hacking" |
| **Medical awareness about overdose** | state prevention and help | the model refuses as instructions |

In all three cases the refusal is **excessive**: the query is legitimate
and the answer has public benefit. Abliteration removes such refusals —
but along with them, the justified ones too.

## How to test in operation

A one-off test before deployment is not enough. Refusal changes with the
prompt, the context and the language. A practical procedure:

1. **A set of 20 queries** from your operation, half of them benign.
2. **A second set of 20** borderline ones, where you want an answer
   with a warning.
3. **A third set of 20** clearly harmful ones, where you want a
   refusal.
4. **A protocol** — who evaluates and on what basis.
5. **A monthly repeat** after every change of model or prompt.
6. **A log** — which queries changed and why.

When the benign set refuses more than 10 %, the model is inconvenient
for your operation. When the harmful set does not refuse at all, you
have an abliterated model and you need to know that.

## Common mistakes

- **Judging by impression** — one query is not a measurement.
- **Mixing languages** — a Czech result does not hold for English.
- **Testing only on public sets** — the model may know them.
- **Not writing down the protocol** — a number without it cannot be
  compared.
- **Confusing a refusal with a tool error** — it is not the same thing.
- **Switching moderation off** after abliteration — the risk remains.

## Where the limit is

- **Refusal is not a guarantee of safety.** A model can answer even
  without abliteration when you steer it there with context.
- **Abliteration does not settle liability.** That stays with the
  operator.
- **Measurement is expensive** — a good set and a scorer are not free.
- **Language and culture** must be reflected in the set, otherwise the
  number lies.
