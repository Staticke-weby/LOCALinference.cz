**RAG** (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) means the model does not get
the whole archive, only the relevant passages that were found for the
question. It is the most common way to bring company documents into
local AI without sending everything into the context window or to the
cloud.

## Short answer

- **RAG = search + generation.** First the relevant documents are
  found, then the model reads them and answers.
- **It pays off from hundreds of documents up**, once sending the full
  text into the context no longer works.
- **It is not magic.** Quality depends on the data, the chunks, the
  embedding model and the checking. Bad RAG is worse than plain search.

## How RAG works

1. **Documents** are loaded from disks, e-mail or a business system.
2. **The text is split** into chunks (paragraphs, pages, sections).
3. **An embedding model** turns each chunk into a vector — a list of
   numbers that represents its meaning.
4. **A vector database** stores the vectors and can find similar ones.
5. **The question** is turned into a vector and the top-K chunks are
   retrieved.
6. **The chat model** receives those chunks and answers with citations.
7. **A check** confirms that the answer matches the source.

Without RAG you send the whole document; with RAG you send only what is
relevant. That saves context, memory and time.

## RAG versus a long context

| Situation | RAG | Long context |
|---|---|---|
| Many documents | **yes** | expensive and slow |
| One large document | sometimes | **yes** |
| Frequent questions over the same data | **yes** | wasteful |
| Exact citations | **yes** | weaker |
| Simple deployment | more work | **simpler** |

Long context and RAG are not mutually exclusive. They are often
combined: RAG selects the document, long context reads the whole
contract. See [context engineering](/topics/context-engineering/) for
how to spend the window.

## What you need

- **A model:** Qwen3.8 Flash Next on a 128GB box.
- **An embedding model:** a smaller model that produces the vectors.
- **A vector database:** FAISS, Chroma, Qdrant, pgvector.
- **Ingest:** PDF, DOCX, e-mail, scans.
- **Checking:** a person who verifies the answer and the citation.

## What RAG does not fix

- **Bad data.** If a document is invalid, RAG will still find it.
- **Permissions.** You have to enforce them in the application, not in
  the model.
- **Hallucination.** Even with context, the model can invent an answer.
- **Model quality.** RAG does not add reasoning the model lacks.
- **Legal responsibility.** That stays with the company.

## Example: contracts in a company

A company has 2,000 contracts in PDF. RAG splits them by article,
stores metadata (year, department, counterparty) and, for a question
like "what is the notice period for supplier X", finds the relevant
articles. The model answers with a citation of the specific contract
and clause. A lawyer then checks only the selected passages instead of
the whole archive.

## Where the limit is

- **Chunking** — bad splitting loses the connection between ideas.
- **Embedding** — quality differs between languages and domains.
- **Freshness** — a changed document needs a new embedding.
- **Permissions** — who is allowed to see which document.
- **Context** — only a handful of chunks fit into the model.
- **Operations** — ingest, backups, monitoring, deletion.

## What it costs

RAG on a 128GB box needs no extra hardware. The costs are:

- **time to prepare the data** — the largest item,
- **the embedding model** — small, often on the CPU,
- **the vector database** — disk space and RAM,
- **administration** — updates, backups, permissions.

A detailed breakdown is in [the cost of local
AI](/topics/cost-of-local-ai/).

## A typical project in five steps

RAG cannot be bought; it has to be built. The order of the steps
decides whether the project ends as a demo or in production:

1. **Pick 50 documents** and 20 questions whose answers you know. This
   is the test set; without it there is no point tuning anything.
2. **Process the text** — extraction, OCR, metadata. The quality of the
   input decides everything downstream (see [PDF and
   scans](/topics/rag/pdf-and-scans/)).
3. **Split and index** — chunking by structure, an embedding model, a
   vector database ([chunking](/topics/rag/chunking/)).
4. **Connect the model and citations** — the answer must show the
   document and page, not just claim to know.
5. **Measure, and only then expand** — Recall@5, faithfulness, refusals
   ([citations and
   evaluation](/topics/rag/citations-and-evaluation/)).

The most common failure is skipping steps 1 and 5: the team points RAG
at the whole archive, nobody measures quality, and a month later no one
can tell what improved.

## What to watch in production

- **New documents** — without ingest, RAG answers from old data.
- **Deleted documents** — their vectors must disappear too.
- **Changed documents** — a new version needs a new embedding.
- **Permissions** — the filter must apply on every single query.
- **Quality** — a running sample of answers, not a one-off test.
- **Context cost** — more chunks mean a longer prefill and slower
  [TTFT](/dictionary/performance/ttft/).

Monitoring and alerting for all of this is covered in
[monitoring](/topics/operations/monitoring/).

## When RAG makes no sense

- You have **one document** that fits into the context.
- You are looking for **an exact code or number** — full-text search is
  better.
- The data is **public** and you do not mind the cloud.
- You have **no one to check and maintain** it.
